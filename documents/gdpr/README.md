# Subject access requests — three drafts, ready to send

Backlog **#67**, whose own rationale set the split: *"I draft, you send."*

Three letters are in this folder. Each asks one organisation for the personal data it
holds about you. **You fill in the bracketed fields, attach ID, and send.** Nothing here
has been sent, and nothing can be — sending as you is not something a session does.

---

## What a subject access request actually is

A right under **UK GDPR Article 15** and the **Data Protection Act 2018**. In plain terms:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | Free. They may only charge if a request is "manifestly unfounded or excessive", which a first request never is. |
| **Deadline** | **One calendar month** from the day they receive it — or from the day they receive your ID or a clarification they reasonably asked for, whichever is later. |
| **Extension** | They may extend by up to two further months for genuinely complex requests, but they must **tell you inside the first month**. Silence is not an extension. |
| **Format** | Usually a copy of the data plus an explanation of what they hold and why. Electronic is normal if you asked electronically. |
| **If they refuse or ignore you** | Complain to the **ICO** at ico.org.uk/make-a-complaint. Free, and you do not need a solicitor. |

**You do not have to give a reason.** A SAR does not require you to justify why you want
your data, and being asked for a reason is not a lawful precondition.

## Before you send — four things that decide whether this goes smoothly

**1. Send it to the right place.** Every organisation has a Data Protection Officer or a
privacy team, and a letter to a general enquiries address can sit for weeks before it is
recognised as a SAR. Search *"[organisation] subject access request"* — nearly all
publish a dedicated address or form.

**2. Say the words.** Each letter states plainly that it is a subject access request
under Article 15. That matters: a vague "please send me my records" can be logged as
general correspondence and never starts the clock.

**3. Expect an ID check, and send it in the same breath.** They may lawfully ask you to
prove who you are, and the month does not start until they have it. Sending ID with the
request removes a round trip that routinely costs two weeks. Photo ID plus a proof of
address is the usual pair.

**4. Record the date you sent each one.** The deadline is measured from receipt, so
keep the sent date and any acknowledgement. If you send by post, consider a service with
proof of delivery — for the police one especially.

## What you may not get, and why that is not necessarily a refusal

This is worth knowing in advance so a partial response does not read as stonewalling:

- **Third-party data.** If your record names another person, they can redact that person
  unless it is reasonable to disclose. Expect blacked-out sections in social services
  files particularly, since those records are often about several people at once.
- **Serious harm (medical).** A clinician may withhold information likely to cause
  serious harm to your physical or mental health. This is meant to be narrow, not a
  general escape hatch.
- **Crime and investigations (police).** Schedule 2 of the DPA 2018 exempts data where
  disclosure would prejudice the prevention or detection of crime. Intelligence-held
  material is often withheld on that basis.
- **Legal privilege**, and material where another exemption genuinely applies.

**A refusal must be explained.** If they withhold something, they should tell you they
have and broadly why. "Here is your file" with silent gaps is not compliant, and that is
the thing worth pushing back on.

## After you send

Diary each one for **one month and one day** from the send date. If nothing has arrived
and no extension was claimed, that is the point to chase once and then complain to the
ICO. Mission Control's schedule module is the natural place to put those three dates —
they are dates somebody else's clock sets, which is exactly what the schedule owns.

**This is a description of a standard statutory process, not legal advice.** If a
response raises something significant, that is the point to get proper advice rather
than to rely on a summary written into a folder.
