# Subject access request — social services

**Send to:** the **council's** Data Protection Officer, not the social work team. The
council is the data controller; a letter to a team can sit in a caseworker's tray without
ever being logged as a SAR. Search *"[council name] subject access request"* — most have
a form or a dedicated `dataprotection@` address.

**If you were in care or had a children's services file as a child**, say so explicitly
(there is a line for it below). Historic records are often held separately from current
systems, sometimes off-site or on microfiche, and a request that does not mention them
frequently comes back "no records held" when records do exist.

## Notes before you send

**Point 3 is the one that earns its place.** "No records held" is a common first answer
when only the live system was searched. Asking them to confirm whether archived and paper
records were searched turns a silent gap into a statement you can challenge.

**Expect heavy redaction, and know why.** Social care records are frequently *about*
several people — family members, reporters, other professionals — and third-party data
can lawfully be removed. The sentence asking them to redact rather than withhold whole
documents is deliberate: it is the difference between a page with black bars and a page
that simply is not there.

**This can be a difficult thing to read.** Care and social work records are written in
professional shorthand, often at a distressing time, and they are not written to be read
by the person they are about. If you request them, it is worth deciding in advance who
you would talk to afterwards if what arrives is hard. That is not a reason not to ask —
it is your record and you are entitled to it — but it is worth not being ambushed by.

**Access to Records services exist** for care-experienced people at many councils, and
they are usually better than a bare SAR: a named person who will sit with you and go
through the file. If point 2 applies to you, ask whether the council has one.
